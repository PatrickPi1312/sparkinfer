The question of how ships found their way across open water before the invention of accurate timekeeping is one of those problems that looks simple until you try to solve it. Latitude was never the difficulty. A navigator with a quadrant and a clear horizon could measure the angle of the sun at local noon, consult a table of declinations, and know how far north or south of the equator the vessel lay to within a few miles. Sailors had been doing this competently for centuries. Longitude was the wall everyone ran into. Because the earth turns, every position on it has its own local noon, and the only way to know how far east or west you have travelled is to compare the time where you are against the time somewhere else at the same instant. That comparison requires carrying a reference clock, and a pendulum clock aboard a rolling ship in a warm sea is worse than useless: the pendulum swings wrong when the deck heaves, the metal expands in the tropics, and the errors accumulate silently.

The consequences were not academic. Fleets ran aground on coasts their captains believed were still a day distant. Ships provisioned for a known crossing wandered for weeks and lost crews to scurvy. In 1707 a British squadron returning from Gibraltar misjudged its position and struck the rocks off the Isles of Scilly, and the resulting loss of four ships and something close to two thousand men made the problem a matter of state rather than seamanship. Parliament eventually offered a prize, and the prize attracted the attention of an obscure Yorkshire carpenter with an unusual talent for building clocks out of wood.

John Harrison's approach was not to protect a pendulum from the sea but to abandon the pendulum entirely. His early sea clocks used paired balances linked by springs, arranged so that the motion of the ship acted on both in opposite directions and cancelled out. He solved the problem of temperature by combining brass and steel in a single strip, exploiting the fact that the two metals expand at different rates so that the composite bends in a way that compensates for the change. He designed an escapement that required almost no lubrication, because he understood that oil thickens in the cold and thins in the heat and that a clock which depends on the viscosity of oil is a clock that depends on the weather. Each of these ideas was individually clever. Assembled together, and refined over four decades, they produced an instrument that kept time to within a few seconds across a voyage to Jamaica.

What is striking about the story, read at a distance, is how much of the difficulty was institutional rather than technical. The board that administered the prize was populated largely by astronomers, and the astronomers had a competing method: the position of the moon against the fixed stars changes quickly enough that, with sufficiently good tables and a great deal of arithmetic, it can serve as a clock in the sky. The lunar distance method eventually worked, and worked well, and had the considerable advantage of requiring no delicate machinery. But it demanded roughly four hours of calculation per fix from a navigator who might also be managing a ship in bad weather, and it failed entirely when clouds obscured the moon. The two approaches were treated for years as rivals when they were in fact complements, and much of the bitterness that surrounds the history comes from that framing.

A similar pattern shows up whenever a field has two ways of getting to the same answer, one of which is cheap to reason about and expensive to execute, and the other expensive to build and nearly free to use. The cheap-to-reason approach tends to be championed by the people who understand the theory, and the expensive-to-build approach by the people who have to live with the consequences. It usually takes a generation for both camps to notice that the sensible thing is to carry a chronometer and also to know how to shoot the moon, so that either can check the other.

Consider a quite different domain: the movement of water through soil. To a first approximation, the ground under a field is a sponge, and the rate at which water moves through it depends on how tightly the pores are packed and how much water is already there. That approximation is good enough to design a drainage ditch and hopeless for predicting a landslide. Real soil is not homogeneous. It is threaded with root channels, worm burrows, desiccation cracks, and the buried remains of older surfaces, and water moving through it prefers these routes overwhelmingly to the fine pores between grains. A hillside can absorb a moderate rain for years without incident and then fail catastrophically during a storm of the same intensity, because a single season of drought opened a crack that delivered water directly to a clay layer forty feet down.

Hydrologists have a name for this: preferential flow. The uncomfortable feature of preferential flow is that the structures which carry it are invisible from the surface, unstable over time, and effectively impossible to survey without destroying them. You can dig a trench and map every channel in the exposed face, and you will have learned a great deal about a hillside that no longer exists in the state you measured. This makes the field unusually dependent on indirect inference, on tracers and geophysical imaging and the patient statistical study of failures after the fact. It also makes it a field in which confident predictions should be regarded with suspicion, and in which the honest answer to many questions is a probability distribution rather than a number.

The same epistemic humility is warranted in the study of animal navigation, where the mechanisms have proven far stranger than anyone expected. That birds migrate was never in doubt; where they were going, and how they knew, took a long time to establish, partly because the obvious hypotheses were wrong. Early naturalists supposed that swallows hibernated in mud at the bottom of ponds, which sounds absurd now but was a reasonable inference from the observation that they vanished in autumn and reappeared in spring and were sometimes seen skimming the surface of water in large numbers before they disappeared. It took the recovery of ringed birds thousands of miles away to settle the matter.

Having settled where, the question of how opened onto a series of surprises. Some species navigate by the sun, correcting for its movement across the sky using an internal clock, which can be demonstrated by shifting a bird's day-night cycle and watching it depart at a predictably wrong angle. Some use the pattern of polarised light at dusk, which encodes the position of the sun even after it has set. Some read the rotation of the night sky, learning as juveniles which point stays fixed, so that a bird raised under an artificial sky rotating about the wrong star will orient itself with respect to that star for life. And some appear to sense the earth's magnetic field, though the receptor responsible remains a matter of argument. One leading proposal involves a light-sensitive protein in the retina in which an absorbed photon produces a pair of radicals whose spins evolve differently depending on the ambient magnetic field, so that the bird may quite literally see the field as a pattern superimposed on its visual world. Another points to crystals of magnetite in the upper beak. The evidence does not cleanly favour either, and it is entirely possible that different species solved the problem differently, or that a single species uses both and cross-checks.

That last possibility deserves more attention than it usually receives. There is a strong pull toward assuming that evolution converges on one best mechanism, when in practice biological systems are full of redundancy that looks wasteful until the primary channel fails. A migrating bird that can orient by stars, by sun, by polarised light, and by magnetic field is a bird that can cross an overcast ocean at night after a storm has blown it off course. Each mechanism alone would be fragile. Together they compose a system whose reliability exceeds any of its parts, and the cost of that reliability is a great deal of apparently unnecessary machinery.

Engineers rediscover this principle regularly and give it different names. In aviation it is called redundancy and is mandated by regulation. In distributed computing it appears as replication and quorum, where a system tolerates the loss of some fraction of its members by ensuring that any decision is witnessed by enough of them that no two conflicting decisions can both be ratified. In cryptography it appears as defence in depth, the practice of assuming that any single control will eventually fail and arranging matters so that the failure is contained. The underlying insight in each case is the same: a system built from unreliable parts can be made more reliable than its parts, but only by paying for the privilege in complexity, and the payment is never optional.

Where the analogy breaks down is in the failure modes. Biological redundancy evolved against the failures that actually occurred, and is consequently well matched to them and poorly matched to novel ones. A bird with four navigation systems is robust against clouds and moonless nights and has no defence whatever against a coastline of illuminated skyscrapers, which is a failure mode that did not exist for the entire period during which the systems were selected. Engineered redundancy has the same weakness in a different form: it protects against the failures the designers imagined, and correlated failures that take out every redundant path at once are the ones that produce the memorable disasters. Three independent hydraulic systems on an aircraft are three genuinely independent systems right up until the moment a disintegrating engine severs all three where they pass close together behind the wing.

The general lesson, if there is one, is that redundancy defends against independent failures and does nothing about common causes, and that identifying common causes is much harder than counting redundant paths. Counting is easy and reassuring and produces a number that can be put in a report. Identifying the shared assumption that would invalidate every path at once requires imagining how the system could be wrong in a way that nobody has yet thought of, which is not a task that decomposes neatly or finishes on a schedule.

This is perhaps why post-mortems are so valuable and so consistently undervalued. A failure that has actually occurred is a common cause that has helpfully identified itself, and the discipline of examining it carefully, without stopping at the first plausible explanation, converts an expensive accident into cheap information about the shape of the remaining risk. The temptation is always to stop at the proximate cause, because the proximate cause is legible and often attributable to a person, and attributing it to a person feels like an answer. It rarely is. The proximate cause is usually the last in a chain of conditions any one of which, altered, would have prevented the outcome, and the interesting ones are typically several links back and involve nobody's mistake in particular.

Consider the study of tides, which combines all of these themes and adds one of its own. The basic mechanism has been understood since Newton: the moon pulls harder on the near side of the earth than on the far side, and the difference stretches the oceans into a pair of bulges that the earth rotates beneath. This explains why there are roughly two high tides a day and why they are larger when the sun and moon align. It also predicts, quite wrongly, that high tide should occur when the moon is overhead, and that tidal range should be roughly uniform. Neither is true. In the Bay of Fundy the range exceeds fifteen metres; in parts of the Mediterranean it is a few centimetres. Some ports have one tide a day rather than two, and in a handful of places there are four.

The resolution is that the equilibrium theory describes the forcing and says almost nothing about the response. The oceans are not a uniform shell of water free to deform. They are basins of particular shapes and depths, and each basin has natural periods at which water sloshes back and forth, exactly as a bathtub does. When the tidal forcing happens to fall near one of those natural periods, the response is enormously amplified; when it does not, the response is small. Fundy is extreme because its length and depth give it a natural period close to twelve hours. The tide there is not a bulge passing through; it is a resonance being driven.

Understanding this took the better part of two centuries and required treating the problem as one of dynamics rather than statics — asking not what shape the water would settle into if it had time, but how it actually moves given that it never has time. That shift, from equilibrium to response, from asking what a system tends toward to asking how it behaves on the way there, turns out to be the key move in a surprising number of fields. It is the difference between thermodynamics and kinetics, between the eventual and the observed, and it is very often the case that the eventual state is irrelevant because nothing ever gets there.

A related shift in perspective transformed the study of glaciers. For a long time the interesting question was taken to be how fast ice deforms under its own weight, which is a matter of material science and can be studied in a cold room with a press. Ice is a crystalline solid that creeps: apply a sustained stress and it flows, slowly, at a rate that depends steeply on temperature and on the stress itself. Measure that relationship carefully, apply it to a wedge of ice on a slope, and you can predict how a glacier moves. The predictions were reasonable and the field made steady progress for decades.

Then people began drilling to the bed, and discovered that in many glaciers most of the movement was not deformation at all. The ice was sliding. Meltwater at the base, generated by geothermal heat and by friction and by water draining from the surface through crevasses, lubricated the contact between ice and rock, and the glacier slid over its bed like a puck. Worse, from the point of view of anyone hoping for a tidy theory, the sliding rate depended on the pressure of that water, which depended on the geometry of the drainage network beneath the ice, which was itself carved by the water and reorganised continuously through the melt season. Early in summer, meltwater arriving faster than the existing channels can carry it raises the pressure and floats the glacier off its bed, and the ice accelerates. Later, once the channels have enlarged, the same volume of water drains efficiently at low pressure, and the glacier slows even though more water than ever is passing beneath it.

So the velocity of a glacier in August depends on the history of the drainage system since May, and two glaciers with identical geometry, identical temperature, and identical melt can move at different speeds because one of them had a warm week in June. This is not a small correction to the deformation picture. It is a different picture, in which the interesting state variable is not the ice at all but the plumbing, and the plumbing has memory.

Anyone who has tried to model a system with hysteresis will recognise the difficulty. When the response depends on the path taken rather than the current conditions, the usual strategy of finding an equation relating inputs to outputs stops working, and must be replaced by something that carries state forward through time. This is more expensive, harder to validate, and much less satisfying to write down, and it is frequently the only honest option.

There is an old argument in ecology about whether communities of species have this character or not. One school held that a given climate and soil produce a characteristic assemblage, so that if you burn a forest down it will regrow through a predictable sequence of stages and arrive back where it started. The other held that the outcome depends on accidents: which seeds happened to be present, which year the fire fell in relative to a drought, whether a particular herbivore was abundant that decade. The first view makes ecology a science of equilibria and the second makes it a science of contingent histories, and the disagreement is not merely philosophical, because the two views recommend opposite things when a landscape has been damaged. If succession is deterministic, the correct intervention is to remove the disturbance and wait. If it is contingent, waiting may deliver something quite different from what was there before, and getting the original back may require deliberately reintroducing pieces in a particular order.

The evidence now suggests both are right in different places and at different scales, which is unsatisfying but appears to be the case. Some systems have deep basins of attraction and return reliably. Others have several stable configurations and settle into whichever one the disturbance left them nearest, and can be flipped between them by relatively small pushes at the right moment. Shallow lakes are the standard example: the same lake can be clear, with rooted plants stabilising the sediment, or turbid, with algae shading the plants out and fish stirring the bottom, and both states resist change once established. Reducing the nutrient load that tipped a lake into turbidity often fails to tip it back, because the turbid state maintains itself through mechanisms that have nothing to do with the original cause.

This phenomenon, where the path back is not the path in, has been given the name hysteresis in physics and the name regime shift in ecology and the name technical debt in software, and while the mechanisms differ the structural lesson is identical. Systems that can be pushed cheaply into a state that is expensive to leave should be pushed with more care than their apparent reversibility suggests. The cost of entry is a poor guide to the cost of exit, and the intuition that a small cause implies a small effect fails precisely where it matters most.

None of which is to say that prediction is hopeless, only that the useful form of a prediction changes. In a system with a single stable state, the useful output is a number with an error bar. In a system with several, the useful output is a map of which conditions lead where, and an estimate of how close the current state sits to a boundary. That second kind of answer is harder to produce and harder to communicate, and it is routinely mistaken for vagueness by audiences who wanted the first kind. It is not vagueness. It is a more accurate description of a world in which the answer genuinely depends on things not yet determined.

The history of anaesthesia offers a final illustration, because it is a case where the practical technique ran far ahead of any understanding of why it worked, and continues to. Ether was demonstrated publicly in Boston in 1846, and within a year surgeons across Europe were operating on patients who felt nothing, which ended an era in which speed was the surgeon's principal virtue because the patient was awake and held down. The technique spread with astonishing rapidity for the period, and it spread on pure empirical grounds: it worked, reliably enough, and the alternative was unbearable.

What nobody could say was what ether did. The molecules involved are chemically unremarkable and structurally diverse, sharing little beyond a rough correlation between potency and solubility in oil, which suggested that they acted on cell membranes in some nonspecific way. That correlation held well enough to guide the search for better agents for the better part of a century, and it was wrong, or at least badly incomplete. Modern accounts point instead to specific binding at particular ion channels and receptors, which explains why molecules of similar oil solubility can differ enormously in effect and why some produce immobility without unconsciousness or the reverse.

Even now the deeper question is open. Anaesthetics abolish the capacity for experience, reversibly, on a timescale of seconds, and the fact that this can be done at all is one of the more remarkable observations available about the relationship between chemistry and mind. It is possible to state precisely which channels a given agent modulates and still have very little to say about why modulating them should extinguish awareness rather than merely altering it. The gap between a mechanism and an explanation is wide here, and the daily practice of the field proceeds across it on the strength of dose-response curves and monitoring, which is to say on the strength of careful measurement of what happens rather than confident accounts of why.

There is something clarifying about a field that is honest on this point. Practitioners titrate to effect, watch the patient, and adjust, and the accumulated craft of doing so safely represents an enormous body of knowledge that is not reducible to theory and does not pretend to be. It is a reminder that the relationship between understanding and control is not the simple one that textbooks imply, and that it is entirely possible to control something well while understanding it poorly, or to understand something deeply and be unable to do anything with it at all.
